# Calculus 1: Differential Calculus (Rates of Change)

## 1. What Is Calculus?

### Definition

Calculus is the mathematical study of **continuous change**.

It has two major branches:

1. **Differential Calculus**

   * Studies rates of change.
   * Focus of this subject.

2. **Integral Calculus**

   * Studies accumulation and total quantities.
   * Covered later in Calculus 2.

---

## 2. What Is Differential Calculus?

### Core Idea

Differential calculus studies:

> How quickly something changes.

Examples:

* Speed changing over time
* Temperature changing during the day
* Stock prices changing
* Machine learning model loss changing during training

The main tool is the **derivative**.

A derivative measures the slope of a curve at a specific point.

---

# 3. Vehicle Journey Example

Imagine a vehicle traveling.

We measure:

* Distance traveled (D)
* Time elapsed (t)

The graph looks like:

### Phase 1: Accelerating

The vehicle leaves home and speeds up.

Distance increases faster and faster.

The curve becomes steeper.

---

### Phase 2: Stopped at Store

The vehicle stops.

Distance no longer changes.

The graph becomes flat.

---

### Phase 3: Highway Cruise Control

The vehicle moves at constant speed.

Distance increases steadily.

The graph becomes a straight line.

---

# 4. Understanding Tangent Lines

At any point on a curve we can draw a:

### Tangent Line

A line that touches the curve at one point and follows its direction.

The slope of this tangent line tells us:

> How fast the quantity is changing at that exact moment.

This slope is the derivative.

---

## Example Slopes

| Situation         | Slope |
| ----------------- | ----- |
| Slow movement     | 0.5   |
| Moderate movement | 1     |
| Fast movement     | 2     |
| Stopped           | 0     |

---

# 5. Derivative = Instantaneous Rate of Change

For distance over time:

[
\text{Slope} = \frac{\text{Distance Change}}{\text{Time Change}}
]

This is simply:

[
\text{Speed}
]

Therefore:

[
\text{Derivative of Distance} = \text{Speed}
]

---

## Visual Interpretation

### Distance-Time Graph

 ^
 |
 |      /
 |    /
 |  /
 |/
 +------------> Time
-------------------code----------------------

Steeper curve = higher speed.

D(t)

# 8. Second Derivative: Acceleration

Now take the derivative again.

We start with:

| ----------------- | ------------ |
| Distance          | Position     |

# 9. Understanding Acceleration

Acceleration measures:

[
\frac{\text{Change in Speed}}{\text{Change in Time}}
]

---

### Positive Acceleration

Speed increasing.

Examples:

* Pressing the gas pedal.
* Entering a highway.

---

### Negative Acceleration

Speed decreasing.

Examples:

* Braking.
* Approaching a stop sign.

---

### Zero Acceleration

Speed stays constant.

Examples:

* Cruise control.
* Vehicle parked.

---

# 10. Derivative Chain

Differentiation can be applied repeatedly.

### First Derivative

[

]

### Second Derivative

[

]

### Third Derivative

Possible mathematically, but rarely needed in machine learning.

---

Used for:

* Gradient Descent
* Optimization
* Backpropagation

### Second Derivative

Used for:

* Curvature analysis
* Newton's Method
* Advanced optimization

Higher derivatives are uncommon.

*** 52. Intro to Integral Calculus ***
# Calculus 1: Introduction to Integral Calculus

## 1. What Is Integral Calculus?

### Definition

Integral calculus is the branch of calculus that studies:

* Areas under curves
* Accumulation of quantities
* Total change over an interval

While differential calculus asks:

> "How fast is something changing?"

Integral calculus asks:

> "How much has accumulated over time?"

---

## 2. Differential Calculus vs Integral Calculus

Calculus has two major branches:

### Differential Calculus

Studies rates of change.

It answers questions like:

* How fast is a car moving?
* How quickly is temperature changing?
* How does model error change during training?

Output:

* Derivatives
* Slopes
* Rates of change

---

### Integral Calculus

Studies accumulated quantities.

It answers questions like:

* How far did a car travel?
* How much water flowed through a pipe?
* How much probability exists within a range?

Output:

* Areas
* Totals
* Accumulated values

---

# 3. Relationship Between the Two

A key idea:

### Differentiation and Integration are Opposites

Differentiation:

[
\text{Distance} \rightarrow \text{Speed}
]

Integration:

[
\text{Speed} \rightarrow \text{Distance}
]

Think of them as reverse operations.

---

# 4. Vehicle Example

The lecture continues using the vehicle example from the previous lesson.

---

## Step 1: Distance Over Time

Suppose we have:

[
Distance(t)
]

This graph shows:

* How far the vehicle has traveled
* At each moment in time

---

## Step 2: Differentiate Distance

Taking the derivative gives:

[
Speed(t)
]

Now we know:

* The vehicle's speed at every moment

This is differential calculus.

---

## Step 3: Integrate Speed

Suppose we only have:

[
Speed(t)
]

How do we recover total distance traveled?

Answer:

Calculate the area under the speed curve.

---

# 5. Area Under the Curve

Consider a speed-time graph.

-------------------code----------------------
Speed
 ^
 |
 |        /\
 |      /   \
 |    /      \
 |___/________\____> Time
-------------------code----------------------

The shaded area below the curve represents:

[
\text{Distance Traveled}
]

This is the central idea of integral calculus.

---

## Why Does This Work?

Speed measures:

[
\frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}
]

Multiplying speed by time gives:

[
\text{Distance}
]

When speed changes continuously, we add up many tiny pieces:

[
\text{Speed} \times \text{Tiny Time Interval}
]

Adding all these tiny pieces together produces:

[
\text{Total Distance}
]

That addition process is integration.

---

# 6. Mathematical View

### Differentiation

Transforms:

[
Distance(t)
]

into

[
Speed(t)
]

---

### Integration

Transforms:

[
Speed(t)
]

back into

[
Distance(t)
]

---

## Simple Example

Distance:

[
D(t)=t^2
]

Differentiate:

[
D'(t)=2t
]

This gives speed.

---

Integrate speed:

[
\int 2t,dt
]

Result:

[
t^2 + C
]

which recovers the original distance function.

---

*** 53. The Method of Exhaustion ***
# Calculus 1: The Method of Exhaustion

## 1. Introduction

The **Method of Exhaustion** is one of the oldest ideas in mathematics and is considered one of the foundations of modern calculus.

Although it was developed more than **2,000 years ago**, the same core idea is still used today in calculus, especially in **integral calculus**.

The key concept is simple:

> Approximate a curved shape using many simple shapes. As the number of shapes increases, the approximation becomes exact.

---

# 2. Brief History of Calculus

Understanding where calculus came from helps explain why modern calculus works the way it does.

| Time         | Contribution                                                                                   |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ~1800 BCE    | Ancient Egyptians used formulas to calculate areas (early integral calculus).                  |
| ~400 BCE     | **Eudoxus** introduced the Method of Exhaustion.                                               |
| ~250 BCE     | **Archimedes** greatly improved and applied the Method of Exhaustion.                          |
| ~250 CE      | Chinese mathematicians independently developed similar ideas.                                  |
| ~1000 CE     | Arab mathematicians such as **Al-Haytham** used integration-like methods to calculate volumes. |
| 14th Century | Indian mathematicians developed ideas similar to differentiation.                              |
| 17th Century | **Isaac Newton** and **Gottfried Leibniz** independently developed modern calculus.            |

---

# 3. Newton vs. Leibniz

Modern calculus was independently developed by two mathematicians.

## Isaac Newton

Focused on:

* Motion
* Gravity
* Physics

Applications:

* Laws of motion
* Planetary motion
* Gravity

---

# 4. What Is the Method of Exhaustion?

The Method of Exhaustion is a technique for finding the area of curved shapes.

Instead of calculating the area directly, we:

1. Approximate the shape.
2. Improve the approximation.
3. Repeat many times.
4. Eventually approach the exact answer.

---

# 5. Step 1: Area of Polygons

Before dealing with curves, ancient mathematicians calculated areas of polygons.

A **polygon** is any shape with straight sides.

Examples:

* Triangle
* Rectangle
* Pentagon
* Hexagon
* Octagon

---

## Example: Hexagon

A hexagon can be divided into simple shapes.

-------------------code----------------------

    |----|
    |    |
    |----|
     \  /
      \/
-------------------code----------------------

It can be broken into:

* 1 rectangle
* 4 triangles

Rectangle:

[
Area = Height \times Width
]

---

Triangle:

[
Area=\frac12 \times Base \times Height
]

---

Total area:

[
Rectangle + 4\ Triangles
]

Breaking a complex shape into simple shapes makes the calculation much easier.

# 6. Extending the Idea to Circles

A circle has no straight sides.

Instead of calculating its area directly, approximate it using polygons.

Now use an octagon.

Approximation becomes even better.

# 7. The Key Idea

Continue increasing the number of polygon sides.

Example:

* Hexagon (6 sides)
* Octagon (8 sides)
* 16 sides
* 32 sides
* 64 sides
* ...

Eventually:

Polygon with infinitely many sides.

At that point:

The polygon becomes indistinguishable from the circle.

The approximation becomes exact.

This is the core idea behind integral calculus.

---

# 8. Why Is It Called "Exhaustion"?

Each new polygon reduces the remaining error.

The unused area becomes smaller and smaller.

Eventually the missing area is "exhausted."

-------------------code----------------------
Large Error
     ↓
Smaller Error
     ↓
Tiny Error
     ↓
Almost Zero
     ↓
Exact Area
-------------------code----------------------

* Infinitesimals
* Differential elements

Then it adds them together.

This process is called **integration**.

# Calculus 1 - Calculus of the Infinitesimals (Limits and Tangent Lines)

## 1. Overview

This lecture explains the connection between **infinity** and **finding the slope of a curve**.

The key idea is:

* In **Integral Calculus**, increasing the number of polygon sides toward **infinity** gives a more accurate area.
* In **Differential Calculus**, zooming into a curve **infinitely closely** makes it look like a straight line, allowing us to accurately determine its slope.

This concept is known as the **Calculus of the Infinitesimals**.

---

# 2. Core Idea

## Differential Calculus

The goal of differential calculus is to find the **slope (derivative)** at any point on a curve.

At first glance, curves do not have a single slope because their steepness changes continuously.

However:

1. Pick a point on the curve.
2. Zoom in closer.
3. Zoom in even more.
4. Eventually, the curve becomes almost indistinguishable from a straight line.

At that tiny scale:

* A tangent line can be drawn.
* The slope of the tangent equals the derivative.

This is the same "approaching infinity" idea used in the Method of Exhaustion.

---

# 3. Why Infinity Matters

Just like:

* More polygon sides → Better approximation of a circle.

Similarly:

* More zoom into a curve → Better approximation of a straight line.

As the zoom approaches infinity:

* The approximation becomes exact.
* The tangent line accurately represents the curve at that point.

This is the foundation of modern differential calculus.

### The Data:
-------------------code----------------------
Dosage (x) → Forgetfulness (y)

3 → 0.57
...

### We want to find the best line:
-------------------code----------------------
y = m × x + b
-------------------code----------------------

### The Error (Mean Squared Error):
-------------------code----------------------
Error = Average (y_true - y_pred)²
-------------------code----------------------

### The Loss Function for weight `m`:
-------------------code----------------------
Loss(m) = Average (y - (m × x + b))²

## Part 6: How We Use the Slope (Derivative)

### The derivative (slope) tells us the direction of improvement

-------------------code----------------------
If slope > 0 (curve is going up):
    → We need to decrease the weight

If slope < 0 (curve is going down):
    → We need to increase the weight

If slope = 0:
    → We reached the bottom (best value!)
-------------------code----------------------

### The Update Rule (Gradient Descent):
-------------------code----------------------
new_weight = old_weight - (learning_rate × derivative)
-------------------code----------------------

## Part 7: Python Code Example

-------------------code----------------------python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# 1. Sample data
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
y = np.array([2, 4, 6, 8, 10])  # Relationship: y = 2x

# 2. Start with random guess
m = 0  # slope
b = 0  # intercept
learning_rate = 0.01

# 3. Loss function (Mean Squared Error)
def loss(m, b, x, y):
    y_pred = m * x + b
    return np.mean((y_pred - y) ** 2)

# 4. Derivative (slope) with respect to m
def gradient_m(m, b, x, y):
    y_pred = m * x + b
    return np.mean(2 * (y_pred - y) * x)

# 5. Derivative (slope) with respect to b
def gradient_b(m, b, x, y):
    y_pred = m * x + b
    return np.mean(2 * (y_pred - y))

# 6. Training (Gradient Descent)
losses = []
ms = []

for i in range(100):
    # Calculate slope (derivative)
    dm = gradient_m(m, b, x, y)
    db = gradient_b(m, b, x, y)
    
    # Update weights (go downhill)
    m = m - learning_rate * dm
    b = b - learning_rate * db
    
    # Track progress
    losses.append(loss(m, b, x, y))
    ms.append(m)
    
    if i % 10 == 0:
        print(f"Step {i}: m = {m:.4f}, b = {b:.4f}, Error = {losses[-1]:.4f}")

print(f"\nFinal result: m = {m:.4f}, b = {b:.4f}")

# 7. Visualize the loss curve
plt.figure(figsize=(12,4))

# Loss function vs weight m
m_values = np.linspace(0, 3, 100)
loss_values = [loss(m_val, b, x, y) for m_val in m_values]

plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
plt.plot(m_values, loss_values, label="Loss Function")
plt.scatter(ms, losses, color='red', s=10, label="Descent Path")
plt.xlabel("Weight (m)")
plt.ylabel("Error")
plt.title("Loss Curve & Gradient Descent")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)

# Data and final line
plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
plt.scatter(x, y, label="Actual Data")
x_line = np.linspace(0, 6, 100)
y_line = m * x_line + b
plt.plot(x_line, y_line, 'r-', label=f"Final: y = {m:.2f}x + {b:.2f}")
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("y")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Left side (far from bottom) | **Negative** | Increase weight (move right) |
| Right side (far from bottom) | **Positive** | Decrease weight (move left) |
| At the bottom | **Zero** | Stop! Found the best value |

| :--- | :--- |
| **Gradient Descent** | To calculate the direction for updating weights |
| **Backpropagation** | To calculate the error of each neuron in neural networks |
| **Linear Regression** | To find the best fitting line |
| **Logistic Regression** | To optimize the decision boundary |
| **Neural Networks** | To update weights in every layer |

## Part 10: The Key Analogy

### Imagine standing on a mountain (Loss curve) in the dark:

| Situation | In Machine Learning |
| :--- | :--- |
| You're standing on a mountain | You have a specific weight value |
| You want to reach the valley (lowest error) | You want to minimize the loss |
| You feel the ground under your feet | You compute the gradient |
| If the ground slopes downward ahead | Negative slope → move forward |
| If the ground slopes upward ahead | Positive slope → move backward |
| If the ground is flat | Slope = 0 → you've reached the bottom! |

## Part 11: Summary

### The Connection Between Calculus and ML:

| Concept in Calculus | Concept in Machine Learning |
| :--- | :--- |
| Curve | Loss Function (Error curve) |
| Point on curve | Current weight |
| Slope (Derivative) | Gradient (direction to move) |
| Finding minimum | Finding optimal weights |
| Tangent line | Best direction to improve |

### The Most Important Sentence:

> **In Machine Learning, the derivative (slope) is the "compass" that tells the model which direction to improve. The rule is simple: move opposite to the slope until you reach the bottom (minimum error).**
