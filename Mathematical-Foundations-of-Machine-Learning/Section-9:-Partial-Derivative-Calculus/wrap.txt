# 3. What Is Calculus?

Calculus is the mathematical study of **continuous change**.

It has two major branches:

-------------------code----------------------
Calculus
├── Differential Calculus
│   └── Rates of change / slopes
│
└── Integral Calculus
    └── Areas under curves
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 4. Differential Calculus

Differential calculus studies **rates of change**.

For example, imagine a car traveling a distance over time.

If we have:

-------------------code----------------------
Distance → D
Time     → t
-------------------code----------------------

we can calculate the derivative:

-------------------code----------------------
dD/dt
-------------------code----------------------

This tells us the **speed** of the vehicle.

So:

-------------------code----------------------
Distance
   ↓ derivative
Speed
-------------------code----------------------

In simple terms:

> Differential calculus allows us to calculate slopes and rates of change.

---

# 5. Integral Calculus

Integral calculus deals with **areas under curves**.

It can be viewed as working in the opposite direction from differentiation.

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
Distance → derivative → Speed
Speed    → integral   → Distance
-------------------code----------------------

If we have a speed-over-time graph, the area under the curve represents the total distance traveled.

So:

> Integral calculus allows us to calculate accumulated quantities, such as area under a curve.

The lecture postpones the detailed discussion of integrals until **Segment 3**.

---

# 6. From Limits to Derivatives

In Calculus 1, we first learned about **limits**.

Limits allowed us to develop the **Delta method** for calculating derivatives.

The basic idea is:

1. Choose the point where we want the slope.
2. Choose another point nearby.
3. Calculate the slope between the two points.
4. Move the second point increasingly close to the first.
5. The limit gives us the slope at the exact point.

Conceptually:

-------------------code----------------------
Point A ← Point B
          ↓
      move closer
          ↓
     infinitely close
          ↓
    slope at Point A
-------------------code----------------------

This is the foundation of derivative calculus.

---

# 7. Derivative Notation

Suppose:

-------------------code----------------------
y = f(x)
-------------------code----------------------

There are several ways to represent the first derivative:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx
f'(x)
d/dx f(x)
-------------------code----------------------

They all represent the derivative of `y` with respect to `x`.

The derivative tells us the **rate of change of y as x changes**.

---

# 8. Higher-Order Derivatives

We can differentiate more than once.

### First derivative

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx
-------------------code----------------------

### Second derivative

-------------------code----------------------
d²y/dx²
-------------------code----------------------

### Third derivative

-------------------code----------------------
d³y/dx³
-------------------code----------------------

For example, using the vehicle example:

-------------------code----------------------
Distance
   ↓ first derivative
Speed
   ↓ second derivative
Acceleration
-------------------code----------------------

This idea becomes useful later when studying higher-order derivatives.

---

# 9. Important Derivative Rules

Before learning partial derivatives, we need to remember several rules from Calculus 1.

The important ones are:

1. Constant rule
2. Power rule
3. Constant multiple rule
4. Sum rule
5. Chain rule
6. Power rule on a function chain

---

# 10. Constant Rule

If a function is a constant:

-------------------code----------------------
y = 5
-------------------code----------------------

its derivative is:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 0
-------------------code----------------------

Because the constant doesn't change when `x` changes.

---

# 11. Power Rule

For:

-------------------code----------------------
y = x^n
-------------------code----------------------

the power rule gives:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = n x^(n-1)
-------------------code----------------------

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
y = x²
-------------------code----------------------

becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 2x
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 12. Constant Multiple Rule

If a constant multiplies a function:

-------------------code----------------------
y = c f(x)
-------------------code----------------------

then:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = c f'(x)
-------------------code----------------------

Example:

-------------------code----------------------
y = 3x²
-------------------code----------------------

Differentiate:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 3(2x)
-------------------code----------------------

Therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 6x
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 13. Sum Rule

If:

-------------------code----------------------
y = f(x) + g(x)
-------------------code----------------------

then differentiate each term separately:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = f'(x) + g'(x)
-------------------code----------------------

Example:

-------------------code----------------------
y = x² + 3x
-------------------code----------------------

becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 2x + 3
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 14. Chain Rule

The **chain rule** is especially important for machine learning.

It allows us to differentiate **nested functions**.

Suppose:

-------------------code----------------------
x → u → y
-------------------code----------------------

where:

-------------------code----------------------
u = f(x)
y = g(u)
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx
-------------------code----------------------

The important idea is:

> We can break a complicated nested function into smaller functions and multiply their derivatives together.

This becomes extremely important when machine learning models contain many nested operations.

---

# 15. Power Rule on a Function Chain

Another useful rule from Calculus 1 combines the power rule and chain rule.

For:

-------------------code----------------------
y = u^n
-------------------code----------------------

where `u` is itself a function of `x`:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = n u^(n-1) × du/dx
-------------------code----------------------

Example:

-------------------code----------------------
y = (3x + 1)²
-------------------code----------------------

Let:

-------------------code----------------------
u = 3x + 1
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
n = 2
du/dx = 3
-------------------code----------------------

Therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 2(3x + 1)¹ × 3
-------------------code----------------------

which becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 6(3x + 1)
-------------------code----------------------

and finally:

-------------------code----------------------
dy/dx = 18x + 6
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 16. Automatic Differentiation Review

In Calculus 1, we learned **automatic differentiation**, or **Autodiff**.

It can also be called:

* Autodiff
* Autograd
* Computational differentiation
* Algorithmic differentiation
* Reverse-mode differentiation

The important idea is that automatic differentiation uses the **chain rule computationally**.

Instead of manually differentiating a huge function chain, a library such as PyTorch can calculate the derivatives automatically.

---

# 17. Why Automatic Differentiation Is Useful

Machine learning models can contain enormous numbers of operations.

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
x
 ↓
operation 1
 ↓
operation 2
 ↓
operation 3
 ↓
...
 ↓
operation 1000
 ↓
y
-------------------code----------------------

Doing all of the differentiation manually would become impractical.

Automatic differentiation allows us to calculate gradients through these computational graphs.

This is one of the foundations of modern machine learning.

---

# 18. Machine Learning's Four-Step Process

The previous subject introduced a very important four-step machine learning process.

For our simple regression model:

-------------------code----------------------
y = Mx + B
-------------------code----------------------

we have:

* `X` = input
* `M` = slope/model parameter
* `B` = y-intercept/model parameter
* `Y` = true output
* `Ŷ` = predicted output
* `C` = cost

---

## Step 1 — Forward Pass

We put our input through the model:

-------------------code----------------------
X → regression model → Ŷ
-------------------code----------------------

For a line:

-------------------code----------------------
Ŷ = MX + B
-------------------code----------------------

`Ŷ` means our model's **prediction**.

---

# 19. Step 2 — Calculate Cost

We compare the prediction with the true value:

-------------------code----------------------
Ŷ vs Y
-------------------code----------------------

The difference is converted into a cost.

A common example is **Mean Squared Error (MSE)**:

-------------------code----------------------
MSE = average((Ŷ - Y)²)
-------------------code----------------------

The cost tells us how wrong our model currently is.

---

# 20. Step 3 — Calculate the Gradient

Now we want to know:

> How does changing each model parameter affect the cost?

For our model, the parameters are:

-------------------code----------------------
M
B
-------------------code----------------------

So we want:

-------------------code----------------------
∂C/∂M
∂C/∂B
-------------------code----------------------

These are **partial derivatives**.

Together, they form the **gradient**.

Conceptually:

-------------------code----------------------
Cost
 ↓
How does cost change if M changes?
How does cost change if B changes?
 ↓
Gradient
-------------------code----------------------

This is the central topic of this Calculus 2 subject.

---

# 21. Step 4 — Adjust the Parameters

Once we know the gradient, we can adjust the parameters in a direction that reduces cost.

Conceptually:

-------------------code----------------------
Calculate gradient
       ↓
Determine direction
       ↓
Adjust M and B
       ↓
Cost decreases
-------------------code----------------------

This process is called **gradient descent**.

The lecture notes that the detailed mathematics of gradient descent will be covered much more deeply in the final subject on **optimization**.

---

# 22. Repeat the Four Steps

Machine learning doesn't normally perform this process just once.

Instead:

-------------------code----------------------
1. Forward pass
       ↓
2. Calculate cost
       ↓
3. Calculate gradient
       ↓
4. Adjust parameters
       ↓
      repeat
       ↓
1. Forward pass
       ↓
2. Calculate cost
       ↓
...
-------------------code----------------------

Over many iterations, the model parameters can move toward values that produce a lower cost.

---

# 25. Define the Regression Function

The regression model can be represented directly in Python:

-------------------code----------------------
def regression(x, m, b):
    return m * x + b
-------------------code----------------------

This corresponds exactly to:

-------------------code----------------------
y = mx + b
-------------------code----------------------

The function connects our:

-------------------code----------------------
x
m
b
-------------------code----------------------

to the prediction:

-------------------code----------------------
ŷ
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 26. Forward Pass in Code

We can calculate predictions with:

-------------------code----------------------
y_hat = regression(x, m, b)
-------------------code----------------------

This is Step 1:

-------------------code----------------------
X + M + B
     ↓
regression()
     ↓
Y_hat
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 27. Mean Squared Error

The cost function used in the example is Mean Squared Error.

The basic process is:

-------------------code----------------------
error = ŷ - y
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
squared_error = error²
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
cost = mean(squared_error)
-------------------code----------------------

In mathematical form:

-------------------code----------------------
MSE = 1/n Σ(ŷᵢ - yᵢ)²
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 28. MSE in PyTorch

A simple implementation is:

-------------------code----------------------
def mse(y_hat, y):
    error = y_hat - y
    squared_error = error ** 2
    return squared_error.mean()
-------------------code----------------------

This performs:

-------------------code----------------------
prediction
    ↓
difference from true value
    ↓
square
    ↓
average
    ↓
cost
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 29. Automatic Differentiation

After calculating cost:

-------------------code----------------------
cost = mse(y_hat, y)
-------------------code----------------------

we can ask PyTorch to calculate the gradients:

-------------------code----------------------
cost.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

PyTorch works backward through the computational graph.

Then we can inspect:

-------------------code----------------------
m.grad
b.grad
-------------------code----------------------

These represent:

-------------------code----------------------
∂C/∂M
∂C/∂B
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 30. Gradient Descent

The optimizer can then use these gradients to update the parameters.

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD([m, b], lr=0.01)
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
optimizer.step()
-------------------code----------------------

This adjusts `m` and `b` according to their gradients.

---

# 31. Why `zero_grad()` Is Important

Before another training iteration, we need:

-------------------code----------------------
optimizer.zero_grad()
-------------------code----------------------

Otherwise, PyTorch accumulates gradients from previous iterations.

The normal training pattern is therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
optimizer.zero_grad()

y_hat = regression(x, m, b)
cost = mse(y_hat, y)

cost.backward()

optimizer.step()
-------------------code----------------------

This is one of the most important PyTorch training patterns to remember.

---

# 32. The Complete Machine Learning Loop

The complete process becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
for each epoch:

    1. Zero old gradients

    2. Forward pass
       X → model → Y_hat

    3. Calculate cost
       Y_hat vs Y

    4. Backward pass
       Cost → gradients

    5. Update parameters
       M, B → new values
-------------------code----------------------

In code:

-------------------code----------------------
for epoch in range(1000):

    optimizer.zero_grad()

    y_hat = regression(x, m, b)

    cost = mse(y_hat, y)

    cost.backward()

    optimizer.step()
-------------------code----------------------

This is the practical foundation for training much larger machine learning models.

# 39. Full Final Code

Below is a clean minimal PyTorch version that combines the main concepts from the lecture: model parameters, forward pass, MSE cost, automatic differentiation, and gradient descent.

-------------------code----------------------
import torch


# --------------------------------------------------
# 1. Input data
# --------------------------------------------------

x = torch.tensor(
    [0., 1., 2., 3., 4., 5., 6., 7.]
)

y = torch.tensor(
    [2.0, 1.6, 1.0, 0.7, 0.1, -0.4, -0.9, -1.5]
)


# --------------------------------------------------
# 2. Initialize model parameters
# --------------------------------------------------

m = torch.tensor(
    0.9,
    requires_grad=True
)

b = torch.tensor(
    0.1,
    requires_grad=True
)


# --------------------------------------------------
# 3. Regression model
# --------------------------------------------------

def regression(x, m, b):
    return m * x + b


# --------------------------------------------------
# 4. Mean Squared Error
# --------------------------------------------------

def mse(y_hat, y):
    error = y_hat - y
    squared_error = error ** 2
    return squared_error.mean()


# --------------------------------------------------
# 5. Optimizer
# --------------------------------------------------

optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(
    [m, b],
    lr=0.01
)


# --------------------------------------------------
# 6. Machine learning loop
# --------------------------------------------------

for epoch in range(1000):

    # Step 0: Clear previous gradients
    optimizer.zero_grad()

    # Step 1: Forward pass
    y_hat = regression(x, m, b)

    # Step 2: Calculate cost
    cost = mse(y_hat, y)

    # Step 3: Automatic differentiation
    cost.backward()

    # Step 4: Update parameters
    optimizer.step()

    # Display progress
    if epoch % 100 == 0:
        print(
            f"Epoch: {epoch}, "
            f"Cost: {cost.item():.4f}, "
            f"m: {m.item():.4f}, "
            f"b: {b.item():.4f}"
        )


# --------------------------------------------------
# 7. Final results
# --------------------------------------------------

print("\nFinal parameters:")
print(f"m = {m.item():.4f}")
print(f"b = {b.item():.4f}")

print("\nFinal predictions:")
print(regression(x, m, b))
-------------------code----------------------

*** 79. What Partial Derivatives Are ***
# Calculus 2 — Partial Derivatives of Multivariate Functions

## 1. Why Do We Need Partial Derivatives?

In Calculus 1, we mainly worked with **univariate functions**, where one variable determines the output.

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
y=x^2
-------------------code----------------------

There is only one input variable, \(x\), so we can calculate:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{dy}{dx}
-------------------code----------------------

However, machine-learning models commonly contain **multiple variables**.

A simple linear regression model is:

-------------------code----------------------
y=mx+b
-------------------code----------------------

Here, \(y\) depends on **two parameters**:

* \(m\): slope
* \(b\): intercept

Therefore, when asking how \(y\) changes, we need to consider each variable separately:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial y}{\partial m}
-------------------code----------------------

and

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial y}{\partial b}
-------------------code----------------------

These are called **partial derivatives**.

### Key idea

> A partial derivative tells us how the output changes with respect to **one variable while treating the other variables as constants**.

This is extremely important in machine learning because models often have many parameters.

---

# 2. Multivariate Functions

A **multivariate function** is a function with multiple input variables.

The lecture uses:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

Here:

* \(x\) influences \(z\)
* \(y\) influences \(z\)
* \(z\) is the output

So:

-------------------code----------------------
z=f(x,y)
-------------------code----------------------

This creates a 3D surface rather than a simple 2D curve.

---

# 3. Visualizing the Function

The function

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

creates a shape called a **saddle surface**.

It behaves differently depending on which direction we look at.

### Along the X direction

If we hold \(y\) constant:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-\text{constant}
-------------------code----------------------

This produces an upward-opening parabola.

### Along the Y direction

If we hold \(x\) constant:

-------------------code----------------------
z=\text{constant}-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

This produces a downward-opening parabola.

This is the key intuition behind partial derivatives.

---

# 4. Partial Derivative With Respect to X

We start with:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

We want:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
-------------------code----------------------

### Step 1: Treat Y as a constant

When differentiating with respect to \(x\), pretend that \(y\) is simply a constant.

Therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2-y^2)
-------------------code----------------------

becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
2x-0
-------------------code----------------------

because \(y^2\) is constant with respect to \(x\).

Therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
\boxed{\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x}
-------------------code----------------------

### What does this mean?

The slope of \(z\) along the X direction is:

-------------------code----------------------
2x
-------------------code----------------------

For example:

|  x | \(\partial z/\partial x\) | Meaning    |
| -: | ------------------------: | ---------- |
| -2 |                        -4 | slope = -4 |
| -1 |                        -2 | slope = -2 |
|  0 |                         0 | flat       |
|  1 |                         2 | slope = 2  |
|  2 |                         4 | slope = 4  |

So the farther we move from \(x=0\), the steeper the slope becomes.

---

# 5. Partial Derivative Notation

For an ordinary derivative, we use:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{dy}{dx}
-------------------code----------------------

For a partial derivative, we use the curly **∂** symbol:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
-------------------code----------------------

The lecturer refers to this symbol as **"del"**.

So:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
-------------------code----------------------

can be read as:

> "del z del x"

This helps distinguish a partial derivative from an ordinary derivative.

---

# 6. Python: Define the Multivariate Function

We can represent the function in Python:

-------------------code----------------------
def f(x, y):
    return x**2 - y**2
-------------------code----------------------

This function has two inputs:

-------------------code----------------------text
x
y
-------------------code----------------------

and produces:

-------------------code----------------------text
z
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 7. Python: Plot Z With Respect to X

To study \(z\) as a function of \(x\), we vary \(x\) while keeping \(y\) fixed.

-------------------code----------------------
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def f(x, y):
    return x**2 - y**2

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 1000)
y = 0

z = f(x, y)

plt.plot(x, z)
plt.axhline(0, color="gray")
plt.axvline(0, color="gray")

plt.xlabel("X")
plt.ylabel("Z")
plt.show()
-------------------code----------------------

Here:

-------------------code----------------------
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 1000)
-------------------code----------------------

creates 1,000 different \(x\) values.

But:

-------------------code----------------------
y = 0
-------------------code----------------------

keeps \(y\) constant.

Therefore, we are effectively looking at:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 8. Python: Partial Derivative With Respect to X

We already derived:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
-------------------code----------------------

So we can implement it directly:

-------------------code----------------------
def del_z_del_x(x, y):
    return 2 * x
-------------------code----------------------

Notice that the function accepts `y` even though it doesn't use it.

This is intentional: in more complicated multivariate functions, other variables can influence a particular partial derivative.

---

# 9. Tangent Lines

A derivative represents the **slope of the tangent line** at a particular point.

The equation of a line is:

-------------------code----------------------
z=mx+b
-------------------code----------------------

where:

* \(m\) = slope
* \(b\) = intercept

If we know a point \((x,z)\) and the slope \(m\), we can calculate:

-------------------code----------------------
b=z-mx
-------------------code----------------------

Then the tangent line becomes:

-------------------code----------------------
z=mx+b
-------------------code----------------------

For our function:

-------------------code----------------------
m=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 10. Example: Tangent at X = 2

At:

-------------------code----------------------
x=2,\quad y=0
-------------------code----------------------

we have:

-------------------code----------------------
z=2^2-0^2=4
-------------------code----------------------

The slope is:

-------------------code----------------------
m=2(2)=4
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
b=z-mx
-------------------code----------------------

-------------------code----------------------
b=4-(4)(2)=-4
-------------------code----------------------

Therefore the tangent line is:

-------------------code----------------------
z=4x-4
-------------------code----------------------

This demonstrates what the partial derivative means geometrically: **it gives the slope of the surface in the X direction at that point.**

---

# 11. Partial Derivative With Respect to Y

Now we do the same thing in the other direction.

Start with:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

We want:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
-------------------code----------------------

This time, **X is treated as a constant**.

Therefore:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2-y^2)
-------------------code----------------------

The \(x^2\) term becomes zero:

-------------------code----------------------
0-2y
-------------------code----------------------

So:

-------------------code----------------------
\boxed{\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y}
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 12. What Does \(-2y\) Mean?

The slope of \(z\) along the Y direction is:

-------------------code----------------------
-2y
-------------------code----------------------

For example:

|  y | \(\partial z/\partial y\) | Meaning    |
| -: | ------------------------: | ---------- |
| -2 |                         4 | slope = 4  |
| -1 |                         2 | slope = 2  |
|  0 |                         0 | flat       |
|  1 |                        -2 | slope = -2 |
|  2 |                        -4 | slope = -4 |

The important difference is the **negative sign**.

The curve opens downward in the Y direction.

---

# 15. Important Observation: The Partial Derivative Can Ignore a Variable

This is one of the most important ideas from the lecture.

Our original function is:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

Therefore, \(z\) depends on **both** \(x\) and \(y\).

But:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
-------------------code----------------------

doesn't contain \(y\).

And:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
-------------------code----------------------

doesn't contain \(x\).

This means:

> The output \(z\) depends on both variables, but the slope in one particular direction does not necessarily depend on the other variable.

---

# 16. Changing X Does Not Change the Y-Slope

Consider:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
-------------------code----------------------

There is no \(x\) in this equation.

Suppose:

-------------------code----------------------
x=0
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
z=-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

If we change to:

-------------------code----------------------
x=2
-------------------code----------------------

then:

-------------------code----------------------
z=4-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

The entire curve moves upward by 4.

However, its **shape and slopes remain exactly the same**.

Why?

Because:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
-------------------code----------------------

is independent of \(x\).

This is a useful way to understand what a partial derivative actually measures.

# 17. X and Y Derivatives Compared

For:

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

we have:

-------------------code----------------------
\boxed{\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x}
-------------------code----------------------

and:

-------------------code----------------------
\boxed{\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y}
-------------------code----------------------

### Direction X

-------------------code----------------------
z=x^2
-------------------code----------------------

when \(y\) is fixed.

The parabola opens **upward**.

### Direction Y

-------------------code----------------------
z=-y^2
-------------------code----------------------

when \(x\) is fixed.

The parabola opens **downward**.

This creates the characteristic **saddle shape**.

# 19. Machine Learning Connection

This concept is extremely important for machine learning.

A model can have many parameters:

-------------------code----------------------
y=f(w_1,w_2,w_3,\ldots,w_n)
-------------------code----------------------

We want to know:

> How does the cost change if I change one parameter?

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial C}{\partial w_1}
-------------------code----------------------

tells us how the cost changes with respect to \(w_1\), while treating the other parameters as fixed.

Similarly:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial C}{\partial w_2}
-------------------code----------------------

tells us how the cost changes with respect to \(w_2\).

This is the foundation for the **gradient** used in machine learning optimization.

The lecture will later build from:

**partial derivatives → gradients → gradient descent → backpropagation**

# 20. Important Notes and Best Practices

### 20.1 Always identify the variable

Before taking a partial derivative, ask:

> "Which variable am I differentiating with respect to?"

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
-------------------code----------------------

means treat \(y\) as constant.

While:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
-------------------code----------------------

means treat \(x\) as constant.

### 20.2 Think geometrically

Don't only memorize:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
-------------------code----------------------

Think:

> "This is the slope of the surface when I move in the X direction."

And:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
-------------------code----------------------

means:

> "This is the slope of the surface when I move in the Y direction."

### 20.3 Partial derivative ≠ full derivative

For a function with one variable:

-------------------code----------------------
y=f(x)
-------------------code----------------------

we use:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{dy}{dx}
-------------------code----------------------

For a function with multiple variables:

-------------------code----------------------
z=f(x,y)
-------------------code----------------------

we can calculate:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
-------------------code----------------------

or:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
-------------------code----------------------

depending on which direction we're studying.

### 20.4 In machine learning, there may be thousands or millions of variables

The simple example only has two variables:

-------------------code----------------------
x,y
-------------------code----------------------

But a neural network can have millions or billions of parameters.

The same mathematical idea still applies: calculate how the cost changes with respect to each parameter.

# 9. Connection to Automatic Differentiation

The lecture ends with an important connection to machine learning.

Calculating partial derivatives **by hand** is useful because it helps us understand the mathematics.

However, real machine-learning models can have:

* hundreds of variables
* thousands of parameters
* millions or more parameters

Calculating every derivative manually would be impractical.

This is where **automatic differentiation** becomes useful.

The previous lectures introduced automatic differentiation with tools such as PyTorch.

For example, PyTorch can calculate derivatives automatically:

-------------------code----------------------
import torch

x = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)

z = x**2

z.backward()

print(x.grad)
-------------------code----------------------

Output:

-------------------code----------------------
tensor(6.)
-------------------code----------------------

This corresponds to:

-------------------code----------------------
\frac{dz}{dx}=2x
-------------------code----------------------

at \(x=3\):

-------------------code----------------------
2(3)=6
-------------------code----------------------

The important distinction is:

> **Manual differentiation teaches us how the derivative is calculated. Automatic differentiation lets the computer calculate these derivatives efficiently for complicated models.**

# Calculus 2 — Partial Derivatives with PyTorch Automatic Differentiation

This lecture moves from **calculating partial derivatives by hand** to calculating them automatically with **PyTorch**.

The mathematical example remains:

$$
\boxed{z=x^2-y^2}
$$

We want PyTorch to automatically calculate:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
$$

at any chosen point \((x,y)\).

---

# 1. Why Use Automatic Differentiation?

In the previous exercises, we calculated partial derivatives manually.

For:

$$
z=x^2-y^2
$$

we derived:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
$$

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
$$

Doing this by hand is useful for understanding calculus.

However, machine-learning models can have **very large numbers of variables and parameters**.

Manually calculating every derivative would become impractical.

PyTorch's automatic differentiation system allows us to calculate these derivatives automatically.

### Main idea

Instead of manually deriving:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x},\quad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
$$

we tell PyTorch to track the variables and then let it calculate the gradients.

---

# 2. The Basic PyTorch Workflow

The workflow is:

-------------------code----------------------
Create x and y
      ↓
Enable gradient tracking
      ↓
Calculate z
      ↓
Call z.backward()
      ↓
Read x.grad and y.grad
-------------------code----------------------

This is the same general automatic-differentiation idea introduced earlier.

---

# 3. Step 1 — Import PyTorch

-------------------code----------------------
import torch
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 4. Step 2 — Create X and Y With Gradient Tracking

We need PyTorch to track operations involving \(x\) and \(y\).

We do this with:

-------------------code----------------------
requires_grad=True
-------------------code----------------------

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
x = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
-------------------code----------------------

Now PyTorch knows that we want gradients calculated with respect to both tensors.

---

# 5. Why `requires_grad=True` Is Important

Consider:

-------------------code----------------------
x = torch.tensor(0.0)
-------------------code----------------------

PyTorch normally won't track the gradient of `x`.

But:

-------------------code----------------------
x = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
-------------------code----------------------

tells PyTorch:

> Track operations involving this tensor because I may need its gradient later.

The lecture describes this tracking as being **contagious**.

That means operations involving `x` and `y` will continue to carry gradient information.

---

# 6. Step 3 — Define the Function

Our function is:

$$
z=x^2-y^2
$$

In Python:

-------------------code----------------------
def f(x, y):
    return x**2 - y**2
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 7. Step 4 — Calculate Z

Now pass `x` and `y` through the function:

-------------------code----------------------
z = f(x, y)
-------------------code----------------------

With:

-------------------code----------------------
x = 0
y = 0
-------------------code----------------------

we get:

$$
z=0^2-0^2
$$

$$
z=0
$$

So:

-------------------code----------------------
print(z)
-------------------code----------------------

gives a tensor containing zero.

---

# 8. Gradient Tracking Flows Through the Calculation

This is an important concept.

We started with:

-------------------code----------------------
x = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
-------------------code----------------------

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
z = f(x, y)
-------------------code----------------------

Since `z` was calculated using tensors whose gradients are being tracked, `z` also has gradient-tracking information.

Conceptually:

-------------------code----------------------
x ─────┐
       ├──> z
y ─────┘
-------------------code----------------------

PyTorch remembers the operations used to produce `z`.

This creates the information needed for automatic differentiation.

---

# 9. Step 5 — Call `backward()`

This is the key step:

-------------------code----------------------
z.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

`backward()` tells PyTorch to perform automatic differentiation backward through the computational graph.

After this operation, PyTorch has calculated the gradients of `z` with respect to the tracked input tensors.

---

# 10. Step 6 — Read the Gradients

After:

-------------------code----------------------
z.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

we can access:

-------------------code----------------------
x.grad
-------------------code----------------------

and:

-------------------code----------------------
y.grad
-------------------code----------------------

These represent:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
$$

respectively.

For:

$$
x=0,\qquad y=0
$$

we already know:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x=0
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y=0
$$

So PyTorch should give:

-------------------code----------------------
x.grad = 0
y.grad = 0
-------------------code----------------------

---

# 11. Complete Basic Example

-------------------code----------------------
import torch


def f(x, y):
    return x**2 - y**2


x = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)

z = f(x, y)

z.backward()

print("z =", z)
print("∂z/∂x =", x.grad)
print("∂z/∂y =", y.grad)
-------------------code----------------------

Expected result:

-------------------code----------------------
z = tensor(0., grad_fn=...)
∂z/∂x = tensor(0.)
∂z/∂y = tensor(-0.)
-------------------code----------------------

The `-0.` is mathematically just zero.

# 14. A Reusable Function

Instead of rewriting the PyTorch code for every point, we can create a helper function:

-------------------code----------------------
def calculate_gradients(x_value, y_value):
    x = torch.tensor(float(x_value), requires_grad=True)
    y = torch.tensor(float(y_value), requires_grad=True)

    z = f(x, y)

    z.backward()

    return z, x.grad, y.grad
-------------------code----------------------

Then we can call:

-------------------code----------------------
z, dx, dy = calculate_gradients(3, 0)

print(z)
print(dx)
print(dy)
-------------------code----------------------

This gives us the values automatically.

# 15. Running All Three Exercises

We can now use a loop:

-------------------code----------------------
points = [
    (3, 0),
    (2, 3),
    (-2, -3)
]

for x_value, y_value in points:
    z, dx, dy = calculate_gradients(x_value, y_value)

    print("x =", x_value)
    print("y =", y_value)
    print("z =", z.item())
    print("∂z/∂x =", dx.item())
    print("∂z/∂y =", dy.item())
    print()
-------------------code----------------------

This lets PyTorch perform all three calculations automatically.

# 16. What Is Happening Internally?

The important conceptual process is:

### Forward pass

PyTorch calculates:

$$
x,y \rightarrow z
$$

For example:

$$
2,3\rightarrow 2^2-3^2=-5
$$

### Backward pass

Then:

-------------------code----------------------
z.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

causes PyTorch to work backward through the computational graph.

It calculates:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
$$

So:

-------------------code----------------------
Forward:
x, y
 ↓
z

Backward:
z
 ↓
∂z/∂x
∂z/∂y
-------------------code----------------------

This backward process is fundamental to how gradients are calculated in machine learning.

# 17. Manual vs Automatic Differentiation

## Manual differentiation

We previously derived:

$$
z=x^2-y^2
$$

then:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-2y
$$

This is excellent for learning and understanding the mathematics.

## Automatic differentiation

With PyTorch:

-------------------code----------------------
z.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

PyTorch calculates the gradients for us.

### Main difference

| Manual                                | PyTorch                                |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Derive formulas yourself              | PyTorch calculates them                |
| Good for learning calculus            | Good for practical ML                  |
| Becomes difficult with many variables | Scales to complex computational graphs |
| Must perform the math manually        | Uses automatic differentiation         |

The lecture's main point is **not that manual calculus is unnecessary**.

Rather:

> Manual calculus helps us understand what is happening, while automatic differentiation makes the process practical for complex machine-learning models.

*** 82. Advanced Partial Derivatives ***

# Calculus 2 — Partial Derivatives of Cylinder Volume

## 1. Lecture Goal

This lecture deepens our understanding of **partial derivatives** through a geometric example: the volume of a cylinder.

The volume of a cylinder is:

-------------------code----------------------
V=\pi R^2L
-------------------code----------------------

where:

* \(V\) = volume
* \(R\) = radius
* \(L\) = length
* \(\pi\) = constant

We will calculate:

1. \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}\) — how volume changes with respect to length.
2. \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\) — how volume changes with respect to radius.
3. Verify both results using **PyTorch automatic differentiation**.
4. Understand why a partial derivative can represent a local rate of change.

---

# 2. Volume of a Cylinder

The formula is:

-------------------code----------------------
\boxed{V=\pi R^2L}
-------------------code----------------------

We can represent this in Python:

-------------------code----------------------
import math

def cylinder_volume(R, L):
    return math.pi * R**2 * L
-------------------code----------------------

For example:

-------------------code----------------------
R = 3
L = 5

V = cylinder_volume(R, L)
print(V)
-------------------code----------------------

The result is approximately:

-------------------code----------------------
141.37
-------------------code----------------------

So a cylinder with radius 3 meters and length 5 meters has a volume of approximately:

$$
141.4\text{ m}^3
$$

---

# 3. Partial Derivative with Respect to Length

We first calculate:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
$$

Starting with:

$$
V=\pi R^2L
$$

Because we are differentiating with respect to \(L\), everything else is treated as a constant.

Therefore:

* \(\pi\) is constant.
* \(R\) is constant.
* \(R^2\) is constant.

So:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
=
\pi R^2\frac{\partial L}{\partial L}
$$

Since:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial L}=1
$$

we get:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\pi R^2}
$$

---

# 4. Geometric Meaning of \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}\)

Notice that:

$$
\pi R^2
$$

is the **area of the circular base** of the cylinder.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\text{base area}}
$$

This makes intuitive geometric sense.

If we increase the length of the cylinder by one unit while keeping the radius fixed, we are effectively adding one more "slice" of the cylinder.

That slice has volume equal to:

$$
\text{base area}\times\text{additional length}
$$

For a one-unit increase in length:

$$
\Delta V=\pi R^2
$$

---

# 5. Numerical Example

Suppose:

$$
R=3
$$

Then:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
=
\pi(3)^2
$$

$$
=9\pi
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}\approx28.3}
$$

So, with a radius of 3 meters:

> A one-unit increase in length corresponds to approximately \(28.3\text{ m}^3\) of additional volume.

---

# 6. Verify the Result Using Actual Volumes

Let's compare a cylinder with:

$$
R=3,\quad L=5
$$

against one with:

$$
R=3,\quad L=6
$$

### When \(L=5\)

$$
V_5=\pi(3)^2(5)
$$

$$
V_5\approx141.4
$$

### When \(L=6\)

$$
V_6=\pi(3)^2(6)
$$

$$
V_6\approx169.6
$$

The difference is approximately:

$$
169.6-141.4=28.3
$$

which agrees with:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=28.3
$$

We can also compare lengths 6 and 7.

The difference will again be approximately:

$$
28.3
$$

because the radius remains fixed.

---

# 7. Why Is the Change Constant with Respect to Length?

Our derivative is:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\pi R^2
$$

Notice that \(L\) is no longer present.

Therefore, once \(R\) is fixed, the rate at which volume changes with length stays constant.

For example, with \(R=3\):

$$
L=5\rightarrow6
$$

adds approximately:

$$
28.3
$$

and:

$$
L=6\rightarrow7
$$

also adds approximately:

$$
28.3
$$

This is because every additional unit of length adds another cylinder slice with the same base area.

---

# 8. Partial Derivative with Respect to Radius

Now let's calculate:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
$$

Starting with:

$$
V=\pi R^2L
$$

This time we differentiate with respect to \(R\).

Therefore:

* \(\pi\) is constant.
* \(L\) is constant.
* \(R^2\) is the part we differentiate.

Using the power rule:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial R}R^2=2R
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
=
\pi L(2R)
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL}
$$

---

# 9. Important Difference Between the Two Derivatives

We now have:

### With respect to length

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\pi R^2}
$$

### With respect to radius

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL}
$$

There is an important difference.

For:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
$$

the variable \(L\) disappears from the derivative.

But for:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
$$

the variable \(R\) remains in the derivative.

This means that the effect of changing \(R\) depends on the current value of \(R\).

---

# 10. Numerical Example for the Radius

Suppose:

$$
R=3,\quad L=5
$$

Then:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
=
2\pi(3)(5)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\approx94.3}
$$

So at the point:

$$
(R,L)=(3,5)
$$

the local rate of change of volume with respect to radius is approximately:

$$
94.3
$$

---

# 11. Why Is This a Local Rate of Change?

This is one of the most important concepts in the lecture.

For:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\pi R^2
$$

the variable \(L\) does not appear in the result.

But:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL
$$

still contains \(R\).

Therefore, the value of the derivative changes as \(R\) changes.

For example:

$$
R=3,\quad L=5
$$

gives:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\approx94.3
$$

But if \(R\) changes, the derivative changes too.

Therefore, we should interpret the derivative as the rate of change **at the current point**.

---

# 12. Infinitesimally Small Changes

Calculus often deals with extremely small changes.

Suppose:

$$
R=3
$$

and we make a very small change:

$$
\Delta R=10^{-6}
$$

That is:

-------------------code----------------------
delta_R = 1e-6
-------------------code----------------------

We can compare the original cylinder with a cylinder whose radius is slightly larger.

The approximate rate of change is:

$$
\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta R}
$$

or:

$$
\frac{
V(R+\Delta R,L)-V(R,L)
}{
\Delta R
}
$$

As:

$$
\Delta R\rightarrow0
$$

this approaches the true partial derivative:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
=
\lim_{\Delta R\rightarrow0}
\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta R}
}
$$

---

# 13. Python Demonstration of a Small Change

-------------------code----------------------
import math

def cylinder_volume(R, L):
    return math.pi * R**2 * L

R = 3
L = 5
delta_R = 1e-6

V_original = cylinder_volume(R, L)
V_new = cylinder_volume(R + delta_R, L)

approx_derivative = (V_new - V_original) / delta_R

print(approx_derivative)
-------------------code----------------------

The result will be very close to:

-------------------code----------------------
94.2478
-------------------code----------------------

which is the same as:

$$
2\pi(3)(5)\approx94.2478
$$

---

# 14. What Happens If \(\Delta R\) Becomes Larger?

If we make:

-------------------code----------------------
delta_R = 1
-------------------code----------------------

instead of:

-------------------code----------------------
delta_R = 1e-6
-------------------code----------------------

the result will no longer match the derivative as closely.

Why?

Because:

$$
\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta R}
$$

over a large interval represents an **average rate of change**, whereas the derivative represents the rate of change at a specific point.

Therefore:

> The smaller \(\Delta R\) becomes, the closer the finite-difference approximation gets to the true partial derivative.

---

Because we want PyTorch to calculate derivatives with respect to both variables, we need:

-------------------code----------------------
requires_grad=True
-------------------code----------------------

So:

-------------------code----------------------
R = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)
L = torch.tensor(5.0, requires_grad=True)
-------------------code----------------------

This tells PyTorch to track operations involving these tensors.

---

# 17. Forward Pass

Now calculate the volume:

-------------------code----------------------
V = cylinder_volume(R, L)

print(V)
-------------------code----------------------

We get approximately:

-------------------code----------------------
tensor(141.3717, grad_fn=...)
-------------------code----------------------

This is the **forward pass**:

$$
R,L\rightarrow V
$$

The input values flow forward through the function to produce the output.

---

# 18. Backward Pass

Now we can perform automatic differentiation:

-------------------code----------------------
V.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

Then inspect the gradients:

-------------------code----------------------
print(R.grad)
print(L.grad)
-------------------code----------------------

We get approximately:

-------------------code----------------------
tensor(94.2478)
tensor(28.2743)
-------------------code----------------------

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\approx94.3}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}\approx28.3}
$$

---

# 19. Verify PyTorch Against the Manual Derivatives

### Derivative with respect to \(L\)

Manual result:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
=
\pi R^2
$$

With \(R=3\):

$$
\pi(3)^2
=
9\pi
\approx28.3
$$

PyTorch gives:

$$
28.2743
$$

Same result.

---

### Derivative with respect to \(R\)

Manual result:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
=
2\pi RL
$$

With \(R=3\) and \(L=5\):

$$
2\pi(3)(5)
=
30\pi
\approx94.3
$$

PyTorch gives:

$$
94.2478
$$

Again, the same result.

# 20. Forward Pass vs. Backward Pass

The entire process can be visualized as:

### Forward pass

$$
R,L\rightarrow V
$$

Calculate the cylinder's volume.

### Backward pass

$$
V\rightarrow
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R},
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
$$

Calculate how the output changes with respect to each input.

This is the same fundamental idea used in Machine Learning.

---

# 21. Connection to Machine Learning

In Machine Learning, we often have a function such as:

$$
C=f(w_1,w_2,w_3,\ldots,w_n)
$$

where \(C\) is a cost or loss function.

We want to know:

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial w_1},
\frac{\partial C}{\partial w_2},
\ldots
$$

These partial derivatives together form the **gradient**.

The gradient is then used by optimization algorithms such as gradient descent to update model parameters.

The cylinder example is much simpler:

$$
V=f(R,L)
$$

and its partial derivatives are:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}
$$

The underlying mathematical idea is the same.

---

# 22. Important Notes and Best Practices

## 22.1 Always identify the differentiation variable

For:

$$
V=\pi R^2L
$$

if differentiating with respect to \(L\):

$$
R\text{ is treated as a constant}
$$

If differentiating with respect to \(R\):

$$
L\text{ is treated as a constant}
$$

---

## 22.2 Do not confuse a derivative with a large finite change

If:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=94.3
$$

at \(R=3,L=5\), this does **not** mean that increasing \(R\) by exactly 1 always increases volume by exactly 94.3.

The derivative describes the **local rate of change**.

---

## 22.3 A variable can remain in its own partial derivative

This is completely normal.

For example:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL
$$

contains \(R\).

That tells us that the effect of changing \(R\) depends on the current radius.

---

## 22.4 Automatic differentiation verifies the mathematics

PyTorch can calculate:

-------------------code----------------------
V.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

but understanding the underlying mathematics is still important.

You should understand why PyTorch produces:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL
$$

rather than simply treating `backward()` as a black box.

---

## 22.5 Gradients can accumulate in PyTorch

PyTorch accumulates gradients when `.backward()` is called multiple times.

During normal model training, gradients should therefore be cleared appropriately, for example:

-------------------code----------------------
optimizer.zero_grad()
-------------------code----------------------

For simple experiments like this lecture, creating fresh tensors for each calculation is also an easy approach.

---

# 23. Full Final Code

-------------------code----------------------
import torch


# ----------------------------------------
# 1. Define the cylinder volume function
# ----------------------------------------

def cylinder_volume(R, L):
    return torch.pi * R**2 * L


# ----------------------------------------
# 2. Create input tensors
# ----------------------------------------

R = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)
L = torch.tensor(5.0, requires_grad=True)


# ----------------------------------------
# 3. Forward pass
# ----------------------------------------

V = cylinder_volume(R, L)

print("Volume =", V.item())


# ----------------------------------------
# 4. Backward pass
# ----------------------------------------

V.backward()

print("∂V/∂R =", R.grad.item())
print("∂V/∂L =", L.grad.item())


# ----------------------------------------
# 5. Manual derivative calculations
# ----------------------------------------

manual_dV_dR = 2 * torch.pi * R
manual_dV_dL = torch.pi * R**2

print()
print("Manual ∂V/∂R =", manual_dV_dR.item())
print("Manual ∂V/∂L =", manual_dV_dL.item())


# ----------------------------------------
# 6. Compare volumes for different lengths
# ----------------------------------------

R_value = 3.0

V_length_5 = cylinder_volume(
    torch.tensor(R_value),
    torch.tensor(5.0)
)

V_length_6 = cylinder_volume(
    torch.tensor(R_value),
    torch.tensor(6.0)
)

print()
print("Volume at L=5 =", V_length_5.item())
print("Volume at L=6 =", V_length_6.item())

difference = V_length_6 - V_length_5

print("Difference =", difference.item())


# ----------------------------------------
# 7. Approximate ∂V/∂R using a tiny change
# ----------------------------------------

R_value = 3.0
L_value = 5.0
delta_R = 1e-6

V_original = cylinder_volume(
    torch.tensor(R_value),
    torch.tensor(L_value)
)

V_new = cylinder_volume(
    torch.tensor(R_value + delta_R),
    torch.tensor(L_value)
)

approx_dV_dR = (V_new - V_original) / delta_R

print()
print("Approximate ∂V/∂R =", approx_dV_dR.item())
print("Autodiff ∂V/∂R =", R.grad.item())
-------------------code----------------------

# 24. Summary

The main ideas to remember are:

1. **Cylinder volume**

   $$
   \boxed{V=\pi R^2L}
   $$

2. **Partial derivative with respect to length**

   $$
   \boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}=\pi R^2}
   $$

3. **Partial derivative with respect to radius**

   $$
   \boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL}
   $$

4. When calculating a partial derivative, **all other variables are treated as constants**.

5. \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial L}\) represents the effect of changing length while keeping radius fixed.

6. \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\) represents the effect of changing radius while keeping length fixed.

7. Because \(R\) remains in:

   $$
   \frac{\partial V}{\partial R}=2\pi RL
   $$

   the rate of change depends on the current value of \(R\).

8. A derivative represents a **local rate of change**, so it is most accurately interpreted for an infinitesimally small change.

9. In PyTorch:

   -------------------code----------------------
   requires_grad=True
   -------------------code----------------------

   enables gradient tracking.

10. Then:

-------------------code----------------------
V.backward()
-------------------code----------------------

performs automatic differentiation.

11. Finally:

-------------------code----------------------
R.grad
L.grad
-------------------code----------------------

give the partial derivatives with respect to \(R\) and \(L\).

12. The cylinder example is a simple geometric demonstration of the same **forward pass → backward pass → gradients** process that is fundamental to Machine Learning.
